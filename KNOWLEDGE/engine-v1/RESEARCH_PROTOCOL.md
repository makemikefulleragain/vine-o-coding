# How to Research Honestly

---

## Principle

You are listening to twenty years of community voice. Your job is to hear it accurately, not to confirm what you already think should be built.

---

## Source Hierarchy

### Tier 1 — Primary (Highest Weight)
These are the voices of communities themselves:
- Community organisation peak body reports (WACOSS, ACOSS, VCOSS, QCOSS)
- Government community consultation submissions
- Aboriginal Community Controlled Organisation publications and statements (Aboriginal Health Council of WA, NACCHO, SNAICC)
- Mutual aid group documentation and internal tools
- Community cooperative minutes, newsletters, reports
- Academic research with direct community participation
- Infoxchange State of the Sector reports (Australian nonprofit tech)
- Volunteering WA / Volunteering Australia publications
- Community Houses Victoria / Neighbourhood Houses networks research

### Tier 2 — Secondary
Professional analysis of community needs:
- Social sector research papers
- Digital inclusion studies (e.g., Australian Digital Inclusion Index)
- Nonprofit technology assessments (NTEN, Infoxchange)
- Local government association reports (WALGA, LGA)
- Community development practitioner publications

### Tier 3 — Lived Experience (Handle With Care)
Raw community voice — valuable but requires careful interpretation:
- Reddit threads (r/nonprofit, r/community, r/mutualaid, r/australia)
- Forum discussions about community platforms
- Social media discussions about what's missing
- App store reviews of community platforms
- Blog posts from community organisers

### Tier 4 — Platform Analysis
What exists and what it tells us:
- Feature lists and documentation of existing platforms (Loomio, Decidim, CiviCRM, Open Food Network, Hylo, Nextdoor)
- Post-mortems of failed platforms
- User complaint threads about existing tools
- Migration stories (why orgs left one platform for another)

### Tier 5 — Lowest Weight
- Vendor marketing materials (actively discount these)
- VC-funded platform pitch decks (useful only as counter-examples)
- Tech journalism (often reflects investor priorities, not community needs)
- AI-generated content (increasingly polluting search results — verify sources)

## Search Strategy

### For landscape scans (first iterations):
- "community platform needs nonprofit"
- "mutual aid technology gaps"
- "community organisation digital tools complaints"
- "why community platforms fail"
- "digital inclusion barriers community organisations Australia"
- "Aboriginal community controlled organisation technology self-determination"
- "community sovereignty digital platforms"
- "Australian community sector digital transformation"
- "Infoxchange state of the sector nonprofit technology"
- "Ask Izzy vulnerable populations digital accessibility"

### For drilling into themes (later iterations):
- Use specific terms from previous research findings
- Search for the specific platforms or tools mentioned
- Look for the specific organisations or communities referenced
- Search academic databases for cited research
- When PDF sources are inaccessible, search for: news articles citing the report, HTML summary pages, press releases, conference presentations of the same data
- Search for "[report name] key findings" or "[report name] summary" as alternatives

### What to watch for:
- **SEO pollution:** Many top results will be SaaS vendors answering their own questions. Discount these.
- **Echo chambers:** If all your sources say the same thing, you may be in a filter bubble. Search for dissenting views.
- **Survivor bias:** You'll find stories from orgs that adopted tech. The ones that didn't — the digitally excluded — don't write blog posts. Seek them out through inclusion studies.
- **Recency bias:** Recent posts aren't necessarily more valid than older ones. A 2015 forum thread about what went wrong with a platform may be more insightful than a 2024 product launch.

## How to Handle Contradictions

Communities disagree with each other. That's not a bug — it's data.

When you find contradictory signals:
1. **Document both sides** — don't pick the one that's easier to build for
2. **Look for the underlying need** — opposing feature requests often share a root cause
3. **Note the context** — a rural Aboriginal org and an inner-city arts collective may need fundamentally different things. Both are valid.
4. **If the contradiction is deep** — this is likely a values question. Flag it for human escalation. It's above your pay grade.

---

## What You're Actually Looking For

Not features. Not specifications. Not user stories.

You're looking for **patterns of expressed need that existing solutions fail to meet.**

The output of research isn't a feature list. It's a map of gaps between what communities say they need and what they currently have access to.
