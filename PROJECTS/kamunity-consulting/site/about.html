The human behind the doors

Mike Fuller

Photo coming soon

It started on a Glasgow high street in the 1990s. Mike was a chugger — one of those people who stops you to ask for charity donations. What he learned wasn't how to fundraise. It was that every high street is a map of community: who has resources, who has passion, who has money, and how almost none of those overlap in the ways you'd expect. That insight has never stopped being useful.

He studied Quality Systems Management at the Scottish College of Textiles in Galashiels — the kind of institution that teaches you to think in systems before you've had a chance to get cynical about them. The holy trinity he came away with: QA first, then improvement, then innovation. In that order. You can't improve what you haven't measured. You can't innovate on a foundation that's still crumbling.

He's applied that framework in places it was never designed for. Brewing. Mining. Higher education. Disability services. Community sector. Local government. The problems are different. The underlying patterns aren't.

The methodology, not the hero

Mike describes his approach as "post-modern structuralist" — which sounds like academic jargon until he explains it: he uses rigorous structure to serve human outcomes, not institutional ones. The goal is never to produce a report that sits on a shelf. The goal is that the people in the room leave with more capability than they arrived with.

His shorthand for what he's against is "enshittification" — the quality decline that happens when organisations optimise for profit or appearance instead of the thing they were actually built to do. He's seen it in every sector. He finds it genuinely infuriating. That infuriation is, broadly speaking, the engine.

He takes his cues from Deming, Juran, and Drucker — post-WWII quality pioneers who believed that fixing systems was more humane than blaming people. He's sceptical of large consulting firms. He's technology-agnostic. Tools serve intent, not the other way around.

30 years of range

The career arc isn't a ladder — it's a map. Brewing taught him that quality is physical and immediate; if the process drifts, you can taste it. Mining taught him that safety culture and quality culture are the same thing, just with higher stakes. Higher education taught him how institutions resist change even when they're explicitly trying to change. Disability services and the community sector taught him what genuine human-centred design looks like when the stakes are people's lives and the budget is nothing.

The $500k at RAC came from three months of watching where the energy was going. The WA Health hackathon win came from leaving the room and going to talk to actual patients. The youth placemaking project worked because Year 9 students turned out to know exactly what they needed — they just hadn't been asked.

The consistent thing: start with real humans, not assumptions. The second consistent thing: the people closest to the problem almost always already know what's wrong.

The wider ecosystem

Kamunity Consulting is the paid consulting practice. It funds the broader work — which includes building free, community-owned AI tools and infrastructure for the sector at kamunity.org.

The philosophy is consistent across both: communities should own their tools, their data, and their futures. Not platforms. Not vendors. Not consultants who leave the good stuff behind when they invoice.
